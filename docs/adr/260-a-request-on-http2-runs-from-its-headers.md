# A request on HTTP/2 runs from its headers, and its body and its answer are pipes

**Status:** accepted
**Topic:** [framing](../design/framing.md)
**Extends:** [ADR 220](./220-grpc-is-served-over-h2c-behind-a-flag.md) (a call runs before its message is whole, and its budget is held by the call's reads), [ADR 253](./253-an-answer-is-handed-to-the-framing-that-carried-its-request.md) (the `.http2` arm streams), [ADR 227](./227-an-event-stream-fed-by-rooms-waits-where-a-connection-waits.md) (an event stream handed over holds no fiber on HTTP/2 either), [ADR 019](./019-a-request-that-lasts-is-still-one-request.md) (a stream and a body stream on every framing)
**Applies:** [ADR 259](./259-http2-is-a-framing-of-every-request.md), [ADR 017](./017-the-trade-budget-has-four-axes.md), [ADR 062](./062-where-a-connection-waits-is-what-it-costs.md)

## Context

The HTTP/2 connection collects a call whole before it runs it and collects its answer whole before it frames it (ADR 220, ADR 253). That was the right shortcut for a unary gRPC call and is wrong for everything else a request does. On HTTP/1.1 a handler runs as soon as the head is read: `c.bodyStream()` reads an upload of 64 MiB through a buffer of the handler's, `Expect: 100-continue` is answered when the handler first reads, `c.stream()` writes a CSV of any length in pieces, `c.sendFile` sends a file, and an event stream runs for an hour. On HTTP/2 each of those is refused (ADR 259), and once a browser reaches HTTP/2 a route that worked would stop working because the framing changed under it. That is the opposite of what the direction is for.

Both halves have one shape: bytes passed between the call's fiber and the connection's fiber, with the client's flow-control window in between. The connection's fiber is the only one that touches the socket (ADR 220), so a call never waits on the socket; it waits on the connection.

## Decision

**A request on HTTP/2 runs when its header block is whole**, gRPC calls included, as an HTTP/1.1 request runs when its head is read. A `GET` whose HEADERS carry END_STREAM is unchanged. What the client sends after the headers reaches the handler through the **inbound pipe**, and what the handler sends goes out through the **outbound pipe**.

**The inbound pipe is the request's reader.** The connection appends a stream's `DATA` to that stream's buffer, as it does now, and the call reads from it through a `std.Io.Reader`, waiting on the connection when the buffer is empty and the stream has not ended. The body is framed by the transport: `http1.Request` gains a body kind for it that ends where the stream ends, and a `content-length` the client sent is held by the connection to what arrived (ADR 259). What the handler has read is given back: to the connection's budget, and to the client as a `WINDOW_UPDATE`, so a stream holds at most its window of unread bytes and a connection at most its budget (ADR 220), whatever the handlers do. So:

- `c.body()` reads to the end, held to `max_body` as on HTTP/1.1. A body that had arrived whole before it was asked for is handed over where it lies, in the stream's pipe buffer, and is not copied. The buffer is the stream's, on the connection's allocator, and not the request arena's: the call owns the arena, and it can be on another thread than the connection's, so the connection never allocates from it.
- `c.bodyStream()` reads it in pieces, held to its own `max_bytes` (ADR 019); the client sends no faster than the handler reads.
- `Expect: 100-continue` is answered with a `:status 100` HEADERS frame when the handler first reads a body that has not arrived, which is the moment HTTP/1.1 answers it.
- A gRPC call's message is read by the envelope through the pipe: the five-byte prefix, the one message, and a gzip message inflated on the call's fiber, charged to the connection's budget before the copy is made and waiting on the connection when there is no room, where it used to wait in the connection's `waiting` state.
- A client that stops sending a body is held to the bound a chunked HTTP/1.1 body is held to, on the call's read rather than on the connection.

**The outbound pipe is the `.http2` arm writing in pieces.** `streamHead` hands the connection a HEADERS frame, `piece` lends it the stream's buffer and waits until the bytes are in the connection's writer, and `end` sends END_STREAM with the trailers. No allocation per piece: the buffer is the one `c.stream` takes when it opens (ADR 019), lent for the length of the wait, and the connection writes the `DATA` frames straight out of it, as many as both windows allow. A client that stops reading holds the call in that wait until the write deadline resets the stream, and the call's next write fails, as it would on a dead HTTP/1.1 socket. So:

- `c.stream()`, `c.events()` and a streamed answer of any kind work on HTTP/2 as on HTTP/1.1, `live()` and the shutdown signal included.
- **A file is read into frames**, a buffer at a time through the same pipe. There is no `sendfile` on HTTP/2, because frame headers go between the pieces; over TLS, where a browser is, HTTP/1.1 has none either.
- A reset stream wakes its call with the error a closed socket gives.

**A call to a route known never to wait runs on the connection's own fiber** (`Conn.runInline`), with no fiber of its own, once its stream has ended. Whether a route is one is decided where it is registered, never at the call: `typed.knownNotToWait` reads the signature, and a route qualifies when every argument is request data (path params, query, header, body, form, the arena, `Authorization`) or a service whose type declares `pub const nilo_never_waits = true;` (a `cache.Space` does; a database, a store, an outbound client and any silent type may wait, so they keep a fiber), and the answer is not a file. A `*Ctx`, a resolved value, the server's `Io`, a `Verified` (it can fetch keys), `Idempotent` and `Cached` make a route opaque, so it keeps its fiber; **a handler the compiler cannot see into takes the author's word**, `nilo.neverWaits(handler)` around it where it is registered (`app.post("/echo", nilo.neverWaits(echo))`), because a route option on the group would have changed `GroupWith`'s seven parameters for every user, and a declaration on the function is not a thing Zig has. The route also needs no middleware (a middleware takes a `Ctx` and can wait on anything), and a listener it answers on. **The call's pipes cannot park** (`Inbox.can_park` and `Outbox.can_park` off, as with no Engine): a body is read from what has arrived, and a streamed answer is collected and written by the connection after the call returns, so the connection is never waited on from inside the call. A call whose request is still arriving (a gRPC call is headers, then its message) is **held** (`Stream.holding`, `Conn.holds`) and runs when the frame that ends it is read, which is nearly always in the same read as its headers; **before the connection waits for anything (a frame not wholly buffered), every held call is given a fiber and reads the rest as any call does** (`Conn.startHeld`), so a client that sends its message slowly is bounded by the read of a call, as before. **The guard is the net**: the loop's turn (`bulkhead.loopTurnNanos`) is read around the call, it moves only if the fiber suspended, and then the route (`Route.parked`) is named once in the log and gets a fiber from then on; that one call stalled its connection, which is the whole price of a promise that was wrong. A reset or a connection that ends while a call is held drops it, since no fiber has it. The answer is written at once from the connection's fiber, so the stream is never queued for the writer, never posts to the waker, and takes no lock.

**An event stream handed to the connection holds no fiber on HTTP/2 either** (ADR 227). The rooms ring the HTTP/2 connection's bell, and the connection writes what was posted to each handed-over stream as `DATA` when it wakes; the handler has returned. `RoomEvents` splits its loop into the step that writes what is posted (`stream.deliverSeats`), which both framings run, and the wait, which only HTTP/1.1's connection loop does. **A handed-over stream is the connection's, not a call's:** `Ctx.eventsFrom` seats the stream with the stream's own bell (`Http2Events.bell`), writes the head, and passes an `EventSource` to the connection (`Framing.handOverEvents`), after which the call returns. A Room's post rings the bell inside the seat's lock, which sets a flag and pokes the connection (`Shared.poke`, the late-arrival rule of the inbound pipe: a stream that is gone is told nothing). The connection's pump gives the stream its turn in the same rotation as every other, `pump_quantum` bytes at most, formats the event into one scratch buffer a connection owns and writes it under both windows; an event the windows take only part of is held (with a reference) and finished first, so a client's window bounds what a stream holds to the Room's backlog and one event. A stream that stays stuck is reset with `CANCEL` at the write limit, by the same `stuck_since` that bounds a piped answer, and a stream that cannot get memory to format its event is reset with `INTERNAL_ERROR` and the connection goes on; a keep-alive comment is written when the quiet stretch is up and bounds the connection's wait; and a handed-over stream counts against `max_concurrent_streams` and ends, giving its seats back, on a reset, a GOAWAY, the connection closing and the server stopping. A connection that holds only handed-over streams has no call to wait on, so it hands its pages and its scratch back when quiet, as an idle connection does, and learns of a stop as every connection does, from the cancel the Engine's main fiber sends once `drain` is over (`Stop.in_flight` counts a request for as long as it is answered, so a handed-over stream is not waited for): the wait comes back `.closed` with `app.stop` requested, and the connection writes GOAWAY `NO_ERROR` and each stream's end under a cancellation shield (`bulkhead.beginShield`), resetting with `CANCEL` only a stream whose event it could not finish for want of window. A held event is formatted once and its remainder kept from the connection's allocator, an event no window has room for is not formatted at all, and a Room's bell takes no lock while the stream is already rung. **The call that handed a stream over releases its fiber's stack pages before it ends** (`bulkhead.releaseEndingFiberStack`, one `madvise` below the running frame, only when `Stream.events` was set): a fiber that ends puts its stack in zio's pool with every page it touched still resident, the route touched 15 to 16 KiB, and a burst of streams keeps as many stacks as there were fibers alive together, so a pooled stack now holds 8 KiB instead ([the runs](../../bench/result/http.md#the-spread-of-a-handed-over-stream-is-the-engines-stack-pool)).

**A whole answer of 16 KiB or more is written from where it lies only when the framework owns the bytes.** The connection's fiber writes an answer after the route has returned, under flow control, so a body the user's code owns (a returned `[]const u8`, a `Bytes.body`, a `Str`, which can point into service state another request frees) is copied into the stream's arena as every answer was. `sendOwned`, a function of `ctx.zig` that a handler cannot name because it is not a method of `Ctx`, is the call for bytes that outlive the stream: the arena's own output (a value's JSON, a `nilo_write`, a message), compressed output, and a static file the App holds. `Ctx.sendKept` keeps its meaning (outlives the middleware chain) and is copied here. The copy left out is 110 µs on a 1 MiB file ([the run](../../bench/result/http.md#what-a-tls-write-is-sealed-in)).

**A WebSocket stays HTTP/1.1.** RFC 8441's extended CONNECT is not offered, so a browser opens an HTTP/1.1 connection for a WebSocket, which it does by itself, and `c.upgrade` on HTTP/2 is refused with a sentence saying so.

**What was built for the inbound half** (stage 6.1). The wait is `bulkhead.Monitor` (a mutex taken where a refusal has nowhere to go) and `bulkhead.Signal` (a condition parked on it, with or without a limit), and `inbound.zig`'s `Inbox` is the pipe: the connection's `DATA` goes into a buffer on the stream's allocator and the call reads out of it through an unbuffered `std.Io.Reader`; `Inbox.whole` returns the bytes where they lie once the stream has ended. One monitor guards every pipe of a connection, a call can outlive its connection, so the only way a call tells the connection anything is `Shared.poke`, which checks that the connection is still there. **The stream's window is given back as the call reads, a half window (32,767 bytes) at a time**, so a read of a few bytes does not wake the connection's fiber and a stream holds at most its window; **the connection's own window is given back as the bytes arrive**, so a stream whose handler does not read cannot stop the others from sending. A stream asked for whole (`c.body()`, a gRPC message) is *collecting*: its window is given back on arrival under the connection's budget (ADR 220), because nothing will read it out. A gzip message waits for room by `Inbox.reserve`, which parks the call until the connection grants the budget, in order, and the `waiting` state of a stream is gone. After a complete answer on a stream whose client is still sending, the connection writes RST_STREAM with NO_ERROR (RFC 9113 §8.1). A `content-length` is held to the DATA as it arrives and at END_STREAM, and a mismatch resets the stream with PROTOCOL_ERROR and fails the read that is waiting. With no Engine a call runs on a thread of its own (a test, `h2conn.Fallback.threads`) or, for `zig build profile`, inline once its stream has ended.

**What was built for the outbound half** (stage 6.2). `outbound.zig`'s `Outbox` is the pipe: the call opens it at its first write (the head, encoded on the call's fiber, and one `Outbox` in the request arena, 184 bytes) and publishes it on its stream; `piece` lends the call's bytes (the stream's buffer, then the caller's slices, then one slice repeated, which is what `Stream`'s writer hands over) and waits, and `end` waits until the end is written. **The call waits uncancellably, because what it lent is memory the connection reads without a lock**: the connection always ends a lend by writing it or by failing it (a reset, the deadline, the connection's end), so nothing lent is ever freed while it is lent. The connection's turn for a stream writes at most 64 KiB of its piece (`pump_quantum`), and a stream that used its turn and has more sets `out_more`, so the connection flushes, lets the calls run and goes round again before it waits. **The turns rotate**: a round begins where the last stopped (`Conn.pump_next`, after the stream that moved last), so with a connection window shorter than the streams' demand every stream gets its share of each WINDOW_UPDATE and not the first in the table all of them; **whole answers are written before the streamed ones** in a round, because they are finite and the connection's write limit would otherwise run out on one starved behind a stream that never ends. A small answer beside an 8 MiB one ended after 128 KiB of it. **A connection that has more to write still looks at the socket every round**, without waiting (`Waker.poll`: the Engine's `CompletionQueue.next` after a yield, in a `-Dhttp2` build only), because it would otherwise read no `WINDOW_UPDATE`, `RST_STREAM`, `PING` or `GOAWAY` until the answer was over; a look that waited a millisecond every eighth round held one 64 MiB lend to 0.455 GB/s, the non-blocking one lets it reach 4.9. It also calls `overdue` from there, at most once a millisecond, so a stuck stream's deadline is looked at while another keeps the connection busy. The write deadline is `Stream.stuck_since`: it runs from the last time bytes of the stream went and stops when a piece is written whole, so a lend that is large and honestly slow is not cut while it moves, and only a client that takes nothing for the write limit is reset with `CANCEL` (the call's next write fails, and the connection and its other streams go on). A `WINDOW_UPDATE` answers DATA that was written and is not a frame read for nothing, so the flood count (`max_control_run`) spends credit before it counts an update; **credit is earned by bytes, two updates (the connection's and the stream's) for each KiB of DATA written**, so a frame of one byte, which a window of one makes legitimate, earns a thousandth of it, and a client that answers each byte with two updates is counted and sent `ENHANCE_YOUR_CALM`. A stream already reset when its call begins to answer fails the pipe at the connection's next turn, and anything a client sends on a stream whose end is written is ignored, as on a forgotten stream. A call that outlives its connection is held by `Shared`'s count, as in stage 6.1, and a pipe it opens or waits on once the connection has stopped writing (`Shared.dead`, read by the pipe under the monitor) fails at once, since the wake that would fail it later is dropped. gRPC calls get no pipe (`Framing.canStream` is false: `Ctx` refuses by name). A file is read **`min(its length, 64 KiB)`** at a time (`file_buffer`), one lend a turn, which measured 3.2 GB/s against 2.3 for a frame at a time and 0.56 for 256 KiB. **The buffers a connection's file calls hold are charged to a budget of 1 MiB (`file_budget`)**; a file that starts past it is neither failed nor made to wait but reads through a smaller buffer, never under 4 KiB, so stalled downloads hold at most the budget and a floor a stream. With no Engine (`Fallback.inline_when_ended`) the call copies its pieces into an arena list and the connection writes them when it returns; every test that is not an Engine's runs that way or on threads.

**What waits on what is the Bulkhead's.** A call waits on its connection through a primitive in `bulkhead.zig` built on the Engine's mutex and condition, because `spawnLocal` can place a call on another thread; nothing in `h2conn.zig` names zio (ADR 001).

**A call's fiber takes the next call that is waiting, and never waits for one that is not.** The Engine's spawn of a fiber for each stream was about 9.5% of a server's CPU under `h2load -m 10` (near 140 ns a request). A burst of streams on one connection (a browser's page, `-m 10`, a gRPC client's hundred in flight) now costs one spawn: a call whose headers are in is *queued* when a fiber has been spawned for the connection and has not yet begun its first call (`Shared.fresh`), and otherwise it gets a fiber of its own. A fiber (`h2conn.runner`) runs its call, hands the stream back, and in the same hold of the lock that decides whether it ends (`Shared.finishNext`) takes the oldest call queued; it ends when none is. **No fiber is parked for a stream that has not arrived**, so a connection between bursts holds no fiber and no stack, the hard axis of ADR 017 and ADR 062 unmoved. **A call waits no longer than it would on a fiber of its own**, because on one executor (and `spawnLocalExact` is what makes it one; where the server's tasks migrate it fails with `InvalidPlacement` and the connection spawns for every call, as before) a fiber of its own would start when every call ahead had finished or suspended, and that is the only thing a queued call waits for. What lets the connection see the second case is a rule it keeps: **before the connection's fiber waits for anything** (the socket, a write, a sleep in the wind-down), and whenever the next frame is not wholly buffered, it looks at the queue (`Conn.rendezvous`); if calls are queued behind a fiber that has not begun it lets that fiber run first (a yield, which queues the connection's fiber behind it), and if the fiber then parked inside its call (a handler waiting on a service, a timer, its own body) the next queued call is given a fiber of its own and the connection yields again. So a queued call is held up only by calls that are running, as before, and a handler that parks is never waited for. The cost is one yield per burst of two or more calls; a lone call on a connection (`-m 1`) spawns as it did and gains nothing. The rules of this ADR hold: the connection never stops reading while a handler runs (a handler that parks is the only way the connection's fiber gets back its turn); a reset, a GOAWAY and a stop reach a queued call through its stream as they reach a running one (`abortInbound` fails its pipe, its first read says so), and a connection that closes with calls queued leaves them to the fiber that will take them, which hands each back to a connection that is gone and frees it. **A stop is honoured by the queue as by the fibers:** `bulkhead.yield` is a cancellation point and reports `error.Canceled` (the connection's rendezvous and its yield between rounds end the connection on it), a fiber that finishes a call while a stop is requested answers each call still queued as turned away (gRPC 14, a 500 otherwise) and runs none, since the one cancel its task was sent is spent on the call before, and a spawn that fails because the server is stopping answers every call queued, not only the one it was for. An event stream's handler returns quickly, so a burst of them is one fiber's work, and the stack's pages are given back once, when the fiber ends, if any call handed a stream over. Measured, [the run](../../bench/result/http.md#a-fiber-that-finishes-a-call-takes-the-next-one-waiting).

**A gRPC stream's API is not decided here.** Server, client and bidirectional streaming are what the pipes make possible, and the shape a handler writes them in belongs to the roadmap's direction [a stream is one shape](../roadmap.md#a-stream-is-one-shape), which builds on these two pipes rather than beside them.

## What it costs

The budget each part is held to, replaced by what was measured when the stage lands. Stage 6.1, measured against `ab11878` ([`bench/result/http.md`](../../bench/result/http.md#what-a-request-on-http2-costs-when-its-body-is-a-pipe)):

- **A request that sends no body and answers whole**: allocations unchanged, 0 from the second request on a connection (a test). Time in process 30 ns over the 960 to 977 it had, 992 to 1,008 ns: a request whose HEADERS end the stream is not piped, so this is the larger `Stream` a connection recycles and the bookkeeping around the call, which was not isolated.
- **A request with a small body**: **7 to 10% slower in process, which is the figure that went the wrong way**: a unary gRPC call 850 to 860 ns before and 912 to 918 after (+62 ns), a JSON `POST` of 13 bytes 811 to 827 and 897 to 906 (+85 ns). Nothing is waited for in those rows (no Engine runs there), so this is the pipe's bookkeeping. Through a real server (`h2load`, 64 connections, 10 streams each, a 1 KiB body to `c.body()`) the spreads overlap, 1.78 to 1.91 million requests a second collected and 1.73 to 1.95 million through the pipe, so a wait through the Engine on every request costs less than that load resolves, about 100 ns of the 2.1 µs of server time a request takes. `c.body()` on a body that arrived whole allocates nothing and returns the bytes where they lie, by two tests.
- **An idle connection**: HTTP/1.1 on a `-Dhttp2` build unchanged (5,313 bytes at 1,000, 5,197 at 10,000). HTTP/2 after one `GET` **+131 to +139 bytes at 1,000 and +70 at 10,000** (9,429 to 9,560 and 9,352 to 9,423): `Shared` 64 to 80 bytes, `Conn` 408 to 432, `Stream` 392 to 608 with its 208-byte `Inbox`. With the stream left open it is 176 to 314 bytes fewer. A build without the flag is `cmp`-identical, and a `-Dhttp2` one is 14.6 KB larger (`example-hello` 1,116,152 to 1,130,800 stripped).
- **An upload through `bodyStream`**: a client that obeys WINDOW_UPDATE against a handler that reads nothing for 400 ms sent 65,535 bytes, the window, and stopped; the process held 68,425 bytes above idle then and 98,890 at the peak of the whole 8 MiB, which completed. The bound is one window and its buffer however long the upload is, and a test asserts three windows.

Stage 6.2, measured against `2cd425d` ([`bench/result/http.md`](../../bench/result/http.md#what-a-request-on-http2-costs-when-its-answer-is-a-pipe)):

- **A stream**: **no allocation per piece** (a stream of 200 allocates what a stream of 1 does, a test). 15.6 to 15.8 million pieces of 64 bytes a second over HTTP/2 against 29.8 to 30.1 on HTTP/1.1: **half the rate**, which is the frame header and copy a chunk does not have.
- **A file**: 3.17 to 3.19 GB/s over HTTP/2 against `sendfile`'s 6.7 to 7.3 over HTTP/1.1 on loopback, one stream, **45%**. A stalled stream holds its `Stream`, an `Outbox` of 184 bytes, the head in the arena, the piece its call lent (a file's is 64 KiB) and its parked fiber.
- **The rest unchanged**: a build without the flag `cmp`-identical, a `-Dhttp2` one 11,200 bytes larger (`example-hello` 1,131,424 to 1,142,624 stripped), an idle HTTP/2 connection 9,423 bytes at 10,000 before and after, HTTP/1.1 and its `sendfile` inside their spread, the rows of `zig build profile` the same. One 64 MiB lend over HTTP/2 reaches 4.9 GB/s.

**What was built for the event streams** (stage 6.3): the connection reaches the stream only through the `EventSource` it was handed (a state pointer and two function pointers), so a program without `eventsFrom` links none of it; a build without the flag is `cmp`-identical, with HTTP/1.1's path of a program that calls `eventsFrom` unchanged, and a `-Dhttp2` one 5.1 KB larger, a program that calls `eventsFrom` paying 9.8 KB more. A handed-over stream weighs 6.2 KB at 10,000 opened a thousand at a time and 12 KB opened in one step, against 19.6 KB for one parked in its handler (4.9 KB on top of its connection when it is alone on it, against 27.8 KB parked); HTTP/1.1's 5,192 bytes and an idle HTTP/2 connection's 9,423 did not move. 100 subscribers are written 15 to 17 million events a second over HTTP/2 and 18 million over HTTP/1.1 ([the run](../../bench/result/http.md#what-an-event-stream-handed-to-the-http2-connection-costs)).

**The call on the connection's fiber, measured** ([the runs](../../bench/result/http.md#a-call-to-a-route-that-never-waits-runs-on-its-connections-fiber)): the arena's baseline-h2c at 1,024 connections 2.47 to 2.60 µs of server CPU a request before and 1.25 to 1.29 after (2.9M to 5.3M and 5.9M requests a second), TLS h2 3.0 to 1.5, unary gRPC 5.7 to 2.4 at 256 connections. Allocations a request: none added. An idle connection 9,200 to 9,300 bytes before and after (`bench/mem.py --h2 --get`), `park-check` unchanged at one page.

Still to be measured:

- **A file over TLS**: its throughput over HTTP/2 against HTTP/1.1 on the same build.
- **An upload through `bodyStream`**: what one connection holds at most, the budget and no more, while a client sends faster than the handler reads.

## What was rejected

**Running a request when its body is whole, and streaming only for routes that say so.** Two paths, a declaration a route has to remember, and a `bodyStream` that silently became "the body, held to `max_body`" on one framing. The rule that a request runs from its head is HTTP/1.1's already.

**A copy of every piece into a buffer the connection owns.** An allocation or a ring per stream, held for as long as the stream lives, to save the call one wait it has to make anyway for flow control.

**Running every call on the connection's fiber, or the lone one, whatever its route does.** The connection stops reading while the handler waits, and a `PING`, a `RST_STREAM` or a `WINDOW_UPDATE` goes unanswered for as long as the handler takes; flow control on a stream needs the other side reading. A prototype of it with only a guard (the loop's turn read around the call) gave twice the calls at 256 connections, and was refused for this. What replaced it is the paragraph above: only a route known never to wait runs there.

**A fiber parked on the connection for its next stream.** The cheapest reuse (no spawn, no rendezvous) and the one that costs what ADR 062 forbids: a parked fiber holds its stack, 8 KiB at least, for every connection that has ever had a call, which is a byte of idle memory on the hard axis for a saving a lone request never sees.

**Queueing every call behind the fiber that is running.** One fiber for a connection for ever, and a handler that waits on a database holds every stream behind it, which is HTTP/1.1's head-of-line blocking put back into a framing whose point is to remove it. Four tests in `grpc_live.zig` and `h2pipe_live.zig` fail with the rendezvous taken out.

**WebSockets over HTTP/2 (RFC 8441).** A browser falls back to HTTP/1.1 by itself, and a WebSocket handed to the connection holds no fiber there (ADR 062); on HTTP/2 each would need its own flow control on a stream for no saving a client can see.

**`sendfile` on HTTP/2 over plain TCP**, a header written and then the payload sent from the file per frame. Possible, and an optimisation for h2c without TLS, which is not where a static-heavy site meets browsers; it is in [`todo.md`](../todo.md) for the session that measures it.
